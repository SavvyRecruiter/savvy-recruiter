Most outreach is one email. Then a hope.

A recruiter writes a spec email on Monday, sends it to a hiring manager they want to work with, and waits. By Friday they haven't heard back. They write it off. By the following Monday they're sending the same email to a different hiring manager. Same cycle. Same hope. Same silence.

The desks at First2 Group booking the most BD meetings aren't sending more outreach. They're running it as a defined campaign — a start, an end, multi-channel, every day for twelve days. The first email does the heavy lifting. The next five touches are follow-up. The phone runs alongside everything.

This playbook is that campaign, written down so any recruiter on a desk can run it the same way every time. When someone says they've "taken a candidate to market," everyone should know what that means.

## A defined campaign has a start, an end, and a plan for every day

The single shift that separates a campaign from a hope is queuing the full sequence *before* you send the first email.

If you write Email #1 on Monday, the next five touches should already be drafted, scheduled, or at minimum slotted into the calendar. The most common failure mode in BD outreach isn't bad copy — it's the recruiter who sends the heavy-lifter email, doesn't hear back, and improvises. They wait three days, send a one-liner "bumping this," wait another five days, send another, and then drift. There's no end date. There's no plan. The candidate eventually goes stale and the campaign quietly dies.

A defined campaign fixes that by making the entire sequence a decision you make *once*, on the day the spec lands. After that, the work is execution, not improvisation.

## The full cadence — 12 days, 6 touches

```
DAY 01  →  Email 1       (the heavy lifter)
DAY 03  →  Email 2       (short bump)
DAY 04  →  LinkedIn 1    (move channel)
DAY 07  →  LinkedIn 2    (different angle)
DAY 08  →  Email 3       (the proof email)
DAY 12  →  Email 4       (break-up + question)

Calls — every day, alongside everything.
```

Six touches. Two channels. The phone running underneath. Twelve days from first email to break-up. After the break-up, the candidate goes into the three-month re-engagement queue, not the bin.

Each touch has a specific job. None of them duplicate. None of them are "just checking in." If you can't say what a particular touch is *for*, take it out — empty touches lower response rate on every touch after them.

## Day 01 — Email 1: the heavy lifter

This email does the work. Everything else in the cadence is follow-up to this. If Email 1 is weak, the whole campaign is weak — the bumps won't save a bad opener.

Three components.

**Opener.** "I know · I heard · I saw" — pick the strongest signal you've got on the company or the manager. *I know* you just closed your Series B. *I heard* on the engineering podcast last week that you're building the inference team out. *I saw* your VP of Eng's post about the latency problem on transformer serving. One specific signal. Not "I've been following your company" — that's not a signal, that's filler.

**Body.** FAB the candidate — Feature, Advantage, Benefit. The Feature is what they are ("Senior ML engineer, seven years at Anthropic"). The Advantage is what makes that distinctive ("led the post-training stack for Claude 3"). The Benefit is the line that gets the reply ("which means he's solved the exact RLHF stability problem your last hire was working on"). Most recruiters write Feature-only specs. The Benefit is the line.

**Close.** A single, specific call-to-action. Not "let me know if you'd like to chat." Something they can say yes to in one click. "Worth 15 minutes Thursday at 2pm?" — single decision, binary answer. "Happy to send his work on inference optimisation if that's the bottleneck this quarter" — single offer, binary answer. The close is where the reply rate is won or lost.

If those three components are right, the bumps that follow will land. If any are weak, the bumps just remind the manager of an email they didn't reply to for a reason.

## Days 03, 04, 07 — short, purposeful, multi-channel

Length kills response rate on bumps. The first email did the heavy lifting. These three touches keep you in the inbox without re-pitching.

**Day 03 — Email 2.** A short bump. Two sentences. Reference something specific from the original email (not "circling back"). "Wanted to make sure the [candidate]'s spec didn't get lost in your inbox — the inference angle felt particularly relevant given [thing you cited Monday]." Done.

**Day 04 — LinkedIn 1.** Channel shift. The hiring manager who didn't reply to email might respond on LinkedIn because the friction is lower. Connection request with a 200-character note referencing the original spec. Not a re-pitch.

**Day 07 — LinkedIn 2.** Different angle. If they connected but didn't message back, send a follow-up message — not about the candidate this time. Share something genuinely useful: a relevant paper, a piece of market data, a peer hire you've seen recently. Build the relationship even if this specific spec doesn't land. The hiring manager who got a useful link from you on Day 07 remembers you on the next role.

The pattern: each bump is *short, single-purpose, and different in shape* from the one before it. If all three are the same "bumping this" message in different channels, you've spent three touches teaching the manager to mute you.

## Day 08 — Email 3: the proof email

The third email introduces social proof. Pick one of:

- **Peer hire.** "We placed someone with this same profile at [comparable company] last month." Names if you can, descriptions if you can't.
- **Pattern proof.** "Last three hiring managers we sent this profile to all said the same thing — [specific objection or signal]. Wanted to flag it in case it's also true for you."
- **Asymmetric proof.** A specific number — response rate on this candidate's profile, his GitHub stars, the offer he just turned down — that signals *others are paying attention*.

The point isn't to brag. The point is to signal "others trust me with this" without you having to claim it. A peer hire mentioned in passing does more than three sentences of self-positioning.

This is the touch that often converts the campaign — the previous bumps got you in the inbox, the proof email gives the manager a reason to act now rather than later.

## Day 12 — Email 4: the break-up that opens the next door

A clean exit. Three lines.

**Line 1.** Signal you'll follow up in three months. "Going to leave this here — happy to circle back in Q3 if the timing's better then." This removes the awkwardness of the manager feeling like they have to reply *now* and tells them the door isn't closing.

**Line 2.** Ask the one question. "Out of curiosity — what would have made this spec a yes? Even if the answer is 'wrong time,' it helps me brief better next time."

**Line 3.** Sign-off with one line that turns a closed conversation into an open one.

> Even a no is intelligence.

The break-up email has the highest reply rate of any email in the sequence. Counterintuitively. The manager who didn't reply to Email 1 through 3 often replies to Email 4 because the pressure is off — you're not asking them to do anything, you're asking them to teach you. People answer "what would have made this a yes" when they wouldn't answer "are you interested." And the answer they give is the brief for your next campaign with them.

## The phone runs alongside everything

The phone doesn't replace the written cadence. It amplifies it.

Every day during the 12-day sequence, you pick up the phone. Some days it's a call to the hiring manager directly (low success rate, high signal). Most days it's a call to their TA partner, an internal recruiter who knows the manager, a peer at the company who can warm the introduction. The written cadence keeps you in the inbox. The phone is what turns a reply into a conversation.

The most common failure mode I see on this is recruiters treating phone and email as alternative routes — "I tried email so now I'll try phone." They're not alternatives. They're parallel. The written cadence is the surface; the phone is what runs underneath it, every day, looking for the warm path.

## Build the campaign before you send the first email

When you write Email #1, the next five touches should already be queued. Most recruiters wing it after they don't hear back. Top billers run a cadence they don't have to think about.

The campaign is the system. The message is the variable.

If a recruiter on your desk hasn't booked a BD meeting in two weeks, the answer almost never is "write better emails." The answer is usually "you're sending Email 1 and stopping." Run the audit: how many specs did they send last month, and how many of those got the full 12-day campaign behind them. The gap between those two numbers is the gap between hope and pipeline.
