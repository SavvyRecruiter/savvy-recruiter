A staff ML engineer I've known for years forwarded me a recruiter's InMail last week. Three words above it: *"look at this."*

The message was good. Genuinely. It named his GitHub handle. It referenced a library he'd contributed to. It said the words "your work on distributed training caught our eye." By every checklist a 2023 sourcing course would hand you, it was a personalised message.

He replied to me with one line: *"They've never opened the repo in their life."*

He was right. And here's the part that should bother you — he knew it in about four seconds, from a message that did everything the personalisation playbook tells you to do. So how did he know?

That four-second tell is the most important thing happening in sourcing right now. Let me explain what it is, why it just became unmissable, and what it means for every message you send this year.

## First, the floor fell out

Start with the numbers, because they're worse than the vibe.

Generic, templated outreach now lands **under a 1% response rate**. Not "down a bit." Statistically level with spam. Meanwhile the people you actually want — senior ML and platform engineers — are fielding **10 to 30 recruiter messages a week**. Do the math on that inbox. You are not message number two. You are message number nineteen, arriving on a Tuesday, behind eighteen that already got deleted.

Then the platforms moved, almost in unison:

- **LinkedIn** cut open InMail allocations to under 100 a month, down from roughly 800. An 87% reduction. The taps you used to flood are now a trickle.
- **Gmail**, as of January 2026, started running every incoming email through an AI that predicts whether you'll care — weighing whether there's a real relationship, whether it's a solicitation, whether it looks like a thousand other emails the recipient already ignored.

Sit with that second one, because it's the quiet killer. Gmail's filter doesn't read your clever subject line. It recognises a *shape* — "unsolicited recruiting message to someone who doesn't know you" — and files you away before a human gets a vote. And every time someone ignores one of your emails, the filter gets more confident about hiding the next.

For fifteen years the answer to weak response rates was *send more*. In 2026, sending more is the thing actively burying you.

## The word for what broke it

Here's where my engineer's four-second tell gets a name.

Last autumn, researchers at Stanford's Social Media Lab and BetterUp coined a term for a specific kind of AI output: **workslop**. Their definition is sharp — *AI-generated content that masquerades as good work but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a task.*

It looks polished. It's fluent. It has the *shape* of effort. And it's hollow, because the effort was never spent — it was generated. The researchers found something quietly damning: when you receive workslop, the work doesn't disappear. It transfers to *you*. People spend nearly two hours, on average, untangling each piece of it — supplying the context the sender never actually had.

Now reread that recruiter's message to my friend.

A tool scraped his GitHub. It saw a repo. It generated a sentence that *referenced* the repo. Fluent. Polished. "Personalised." And to a man who has received forty of these this month, instantly, obviously hollow — because it had the shape of someone having read his work without the substance of anyone having done so.

It was workslop with a name field. That's the tell. He wasn't reacting to a bad message. He was reacting to the *uncanny* feeling of effort-shaped emptiness — the same feeling you get opening a five-paragraph email that somehow says nothing. Senior engineers have recalibrated to detect it, and the detector is getting faster.

## The twist nobody selling you AI outreach will mention

Every vendor's pitch right now is the same: AI lets you send 100 personalised messages in the time it took to write 40. Personalisation at scale. The contradiction resolved.

But walk the logic one more step. If AI can generate a GitHub-referencing message in two seconds, then a GitHub-referencing message *is no longer evidence of anything.* It used to signal "this person did their homework." Now it signals "this person has the same tool everyone else has." The personalisation got automated, so the personalisation stopped meaning anything.

Which leads somewhere genuinely counterintuitive, and it's the whole reason this newsletter exists:

**The more outreach AI can fake, the more valuable it becomes to prove a human actually did the work.**

When generic messaging was merely *ineffective*, volume could brute-force it. Now that the channels filter for shape and the candidates pattern-match hollowness in a sentence, the only thing that breaks through is the one thing that doesn't scale: proof you specifically engaged with their work specifically. Not "I saw your impressive GitHub." The PR number. The design trade-off in the model card you'd have argued with. The line in the paper you disagreed with. The thing a scraper can't surface because it requires a human to have *understood* it.

The data draws the line precisely. Personalised, specific outreach holds **18–25% reply rates**. Templated sends sit under 5%. A message naming a specific PR or result beats generic flattery by **2–3x** — and after Gmail's change, that gap isn't about charm anymore. It's the difference between arriving in the inbox and never arriving at all.

## Try this on your last ten messages

Here's the test, and it takes ten minutes.

Pull your last ten sent messages. For each line, ask one question: *could a tool with this candidate's profile open have generated this exact sentence?*

If yes — it's workslop, and it's training the filters against you while teaching the candidate to delete you on sight.

If no — if the line required you to read the repo, trace the paper, understand the competition placement — that's the sentence that lands. That's the four seconds working *for* you instead of against you.

Count how many of your ten survive. For most desks, honestly, it's one or two. That's not a failure — it's the whole opportunity. Because three messages a day where every line passes that test will out-reply fifty that don't. Not because effort is virtuous. Because in June 2026, proof of work is the only version the algorithm, the inbox, *and* the human all agree to let through.

My engineer friend never replied to that recruiter. He did reply to the next one — someone who quoted a specific decision from a two-year-old pull request and asked why he'd done it that way.

That recruiter didn't have a better tool. They did the reading. In 2026, that's the entire game.
